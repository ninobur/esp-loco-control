### 2. Relevant target-acquisition principles from established sources
Established techniques from radar, sonar, and magnetic sensing that address detection of known or semi-known signatures in clutter/noise include:

- **Matched filtering / template correlation**: Optimal linear filter that maximizes SNR for a known signal shape in additive noise by correlating the received waveform with a time-reversed template of the expected signal. Widely used in radar pulse compression and magnetic flow cytometry.
- **Constant False Alarm Rate (CFAR) detection**: Adaptive thresholding that estimates local clutter/noise power from surrounding cells (or time windows) and sets a threshold to hold false-alarm probability approximately constant despite varying background levels. Common variants: cell-averaging (CA), ordered-statistic (OS), greatest-of (GO).
- **Measurement gating / validation gates**: Spatial/temporal regions around a predicted measurement (often ellipsoidal, based on Mahalanobis distance) that discard unlikely associations before full data association. Reduces combinatorial load and rejects gross outliers.
- **Sequential evidence accumulation / track-before-detect and multi-hypothesis tracking (MHT)**: Integrate weak detections over time or maintain multiple association hypotheses until later data resolves ambiguity. Includes probabilistic data association (PDA/JPDA) and deferred-decision logic.
- **Clutter / interference rejection and differential sensing**: Spatial differencing (multiple Hall elements), filtering of slowly varying backgrounds, and model-based suppression of known interferers. Documented in railway Hall/magnetic sleeper and wheel detectors.
- **Contextual / multi-sensor classification and data association**: Use of kinematic constraints, expected spacing, or secondary features to classify and associate detections while rejecting duplicates and echoes.

These are facts established in the cited technical literature (radar/sonar textbooks, IEEE papers, magnetic-sensor applications).

### 3. Concrete examples of how useful principles might apply to the locomotive
- **Matched filtering**: Record or model the expected bipolar or multi-peak Hall voltage waveform produced by a magnet (or sleeper bolt) at typical speeds and air-gaps. Correlate the live Hall time-series against this template (or a bank of speed-normalized templates). Peaks above a threshold indicate candidate detections. Directly analogous to magnetic particle detection at low SNR.
- **CFAR**: Slide a short temporal window along the Hall stream; estimate local noise/clutter power from neighboring samples (excluding guard samples around the test cell); raise/lower the detection threshold to keep false-alarm rate roughly constant as the locomotive passes regions of varying magnetic background (e.g., near substations or other trains).
- **Measurement gating**: Once a candidate is detected, predict the next expected magnet location from train speed and nominal spacing. Accept subsequent Hall responses only if they fall inside a gate sized by position uncertainty and sensor noise. Reject out-of-gate responses as clutter or duplicates.
- **Sequential evidence / MHT-style association**: Maintain tentative tracks of successive magnet detections. Score alternative associations (new target vs. continuation vs. clutter). Confirm a track only after several consistent detections; prune low-likelihood hypotheses. Useful for rejecting isolated false triggers or multipath-like magnetic echoes.
- **Differential / multi-Hall rejection**: Use spatially separated Hall elements; subtract common-mode background (Earth field, distant sources) while retaining the localized gradient from a nearby magnet. Combine with temporal filtering of DC offsets.

**Inference**: These map naturally because the “target” produces a repeatable, roughly known signature whose timing is constrained by train kinematics—exactly the regime in which matched filters, gates, and sequential association excel.

### 4. Assumptions required by each technique
- **Matched filter**: Known (or well-modeled) signal shape; additive stationary or slowly varying noise; template library covers expected speed/air-gap/temperature variations.
- **CFAR**: Local clutter statistics are homogeneous enough to be estimated from nearby samples; reference window size trades bias vs. variance.
- **Gating**: Reasonable process and measurement noise models (so predicted covariance is meaningful); targets do not maneuver wildly relative to the prediction horizon.
- **Sequential / MHT**: Ability to maintain a manageable number of hypotheses; kinematic model of relative motion; some statistical model of clutter density and detection probability.
- **Differential sensing**: Interfering fields are spatially correlated over the baseline of the sensor array; target field is more localized.

### 5. Risks, limitations, and likely failure modes
- Matched filter degrades if the actual waveform (speed, orientation, magnetization, temperature drift) departs significantly from the template → missed detections or elevated sidelobes.
- CFAR can produce threshold inflation at clutter edges or when multiple targets occupy the reference window → missed detections; or under-estimation in sparse clutter → excess false alarms.
- Hard gating discards a true measurement that falls outside the gate (especially after a maneuver or large speed change), leading to track loss.
- Hypothesis trees grow combinatorially in dense clutter; real-time embedded processors may be forced to aggressive pruning that discards the correct hypothesis.
- Differential sensing fails against strong local interferers or when the target field itself becomes common-mode at large air-gaps.
- Overall: any technique that assumes “physical magnets are present if software accepts the Hall response” (explicitly forbidden by the assignment) will produce incorrect operational conclusions when software thresholds or filters are the actual source of rejection.

### 6. Techniques that appear unsuitable and why
- Pure optical/LiDAR-style appearance matching or deep visual classifiers: the primary sensor is magnetic (Hall); visual methods address different phenomenology and do not directly solve magnetic clutter.
- High-dimensional multi-target trackers designed for dense, continuously maneuvering airborne targets (full JPDA or large-scale MHT without heavy pruning): computational cost and hypothesis volume exceed typical embedded railway constraints when the expected number of simultaneous magnets is small and kinematics are largely one-dimensional.
- Assuming radar range-Doppler processing or sonar reverberation models transfer without adaptation: the measurement is a scalar (or low-dimensional) magnetic time series, not a high-resolution range profile; clutter statistics differ.
- Treating every threshold crossing as an independent target without sequential consistency checks: ignores the strong temporal and spatial correlation imposed by fixed track geometry and known train motion.

### 7. Smallest experiments needed before choosing an architecture
1. Collect synchronized Hall time-series, train speed, and ground-truth magnet locations (or controlled test magnets) under representative conditions (varying speed, load, nearby equipment, temperature).
2. Characterize the empirical signal shape library and residual noise/clutter statistics (power spectral density, non-stationarity).
3. Implement and compare simple thresholding vs. matched-filter bank vs. CFAR on the same data; measure detection/false-alarm rates and sensitivity to template mismatch.
4. Add a basic kinematic gate and sequential confirmation logic; quantify reduction in false tracks and any increase in missed detections.
5. Stress-test under controlled magnetic interference and extreme air-gaps or speeds to expose failure modes.
6. Profile computational cost on the target embedded platform.

These are the minimal data-driven steps that separate assumptions from observed railway behavior.

### 8. Questions requiring operator judgment
- What is the maximum acceptable false-alarm rate versus missed-detection rate for the operational use case (safety-critical positioning? non-critical identification?)?
- How variable are magnet magnetization, mounting height, and orientation across the fleet/infrastructure?
- What real-time latency and computational budget are hard constraints on the locomotive processor?
- Are secondary sensors (speed, GPS, additional magnetic channels, inertial) available for fusion, and what is their reliability?
- How should the system behave when evidence is ambiguous (report “unknown,” maintain last confirmed state, or request operator confirmation)?
- What is the cost of a software rejection of a physically present magnet versus acceptance of a false magnet?

**Recommendations** (explicitly labeled as such): Begin with a matched-filter + adaptive-threshold (CFAR-like) front end followed by simple kinematic gating and sequential confirmation. This combination is lightweight, directly exploits the known signature and one-dimensional motion, and is well-supported by both classic detection theory and existing magnetic-sensing practice. Only escalate to full multi-hypothesis machinery if the simplest pipeline fails the empirical tests above. Do not infer physical absence of magnets from software decisions alone.
