Executive summary
TEMPLATES asks a narrower question than most target-acquisition literature answers. Classical target detection (radar, sonar, image tracking) usually deals with an unknown number of targets in continuous background clutter, and spends most of its effort on deciding which of several candidate returns belongs to which track. TEMPLATES has exactly one target and one track: the locomotive, and the one magnet the route map says is physically next. The problem isn't "whose is this echo" — it's "does this pulse belong to the only thing being expected, or is it noise." That's a much easier and better-conditioned problem than multi-target tracking, and several ideas below map cleanly onto it. A few others (adapted from image/radar tracking) are the wrong tool for a single-known-target embedded problem and are called out as such.

1. The TEMPLATES problem, plainly
Given position, orientation, and direction, the route map yields one physically possible next magnet — a known polarity, an approximate flux signature, and (once moving from a confirmed marker) an expected arrival window. A Hall response is evaluated against that known target, not classified on its own merits. Three properties are hard exclusions — wrong polarity, below-minimum flux, or physically-impossible-early arrival — and none of them may move the marker count. Everything else (timing fit, flux-pattern fit) is confirmatory evidence layered on top once the hard gates pass. This is a single-hypothesis, known-template detection problem with sequential confirmatory evidence, not a multi-target data-association problem.
2–3. Relevant principles and how they'd apply to the locomotive
Matched filtering. The optimal detector for a signal of known shape buried in noise is the correlation of the incoming signal against a template of the expected waveform — literally where the "template" vocabulary in signal theory comes from, used across radar, sonar, and gravitational-wave detection.
Railway application: if a magnet's Hall excursion has a repeatable shape at a given speed (rise, peak, decay), the ESP could correlate incoming samples against a stored template rather than reacting to a bare threshold crossing. This is the technically correct answer to "how do I know I've found the real peak, not just an early rising edge" — but it requires the waveform shape to actually be stable across passes, which is presently unverified (see unresolved question 5 in the interview record).
Assumption: the pulse shape must be known and reasonably repeatable; the noise must be roughly stationary. If PWM/speed changes stretch or shrink the pulse shape substantially, a single fixed template won't correlate well across speeds — you'd need a small template bank per PWM/speed band, which adds real complexity for an ESP32.
Measurement gating (validation gates). In Kalman-filter tracking, a "gate" is a region in measurement space — built from the predicted state and its uncertainty — outside of which a candidate measurement is excluded from consideration before any scoring happens.
Railway application: this is precisely what the three hard gates already are — polarity gate, flux-minimum gate, and time-minimum gate — expressed in tracking language. The interview record's instinct to treat these as absolute pre-filters (not soft evidence) matches how gating is used in that literature: gating is deliberately binary (in/out), and only what survives the gate gets scored further.
Assumption: the gate boundaries (minimum interval, minimum flux) must be measured, not guessed — which is exactly what Questions 15–21 are already doing.
Nearest-neighbor vs. probabilistic data association (JPDA/PDAF). These solve a different problem than yours by default: what to do when more than one candidate measurement survives the gate at the same time. Plain nearest-neighbor picks the best-scoring one and discards the rest; PDA/JPDA instead blend all surviving candidates weighted by their probability of being real, which noticeably outperforms nearest-neighbor once more than one spurious return can appear inside a single gate.
Railway application: this class of technique is only relevant if TEMPLATES can face two candidate excursions inside one valid gate window — e.g., a real magnet pass plus a vibration-induced blip of the same polarity, in the same window. If that scenario is rare or physically implausible on your track, full JPDA machinery is overkill; the operator's stated approach (record evidence categories, don't jump to a weighted score) is closer to gating-then-nearest-neighbor than to probabilistic blending, and that's a reasonable starting point for a single-target system.
Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT). A sequential method that accumulates a likelihood ratio observation-by-observation and stops as soon as enough evidence has piled up to decide between two hypotheses, without needing a pre-fixed sample count.
Railway application: two places this could genuinely help: (a) deciding when a Hall excursion has actually closed (accumulate evidence that the signal has returned to baseline rather than reacting to the first near-zero sample, directly addressing Question 8/9), and (b) deciding whether a "provisional" marker hit (passed hard gates, lacking full timing confirmation) should be promoted to confirmed as subsequent evidence arrives — which is exactly the open Question 12 in your record. SPRT gives a principled, tunable way to do that instead of an ad hoc rule.
Assumption: SPRT needs a model of what values look like under "real event" vs. "still noise" — i.e., you need at least rough distributions for the confirmatory evidence, which you don't have yet.
CFAR (constant false alarm rate) / adaptive thresholding. Instead of using a fixed detection threshold, CFAR estimates the local noise/clutter level from surrounding samples or cells and adjusts the threshold to hold the false-alarm rate constant as conditions change.
Railway application: the underlying idea — don't use a single fixed flux threshold everywhere, adapt it to locally observed baseline noise — is worth keeping in mind if ambient magnetic noise (motor fields, other rolling stock) varies meaningfully by section of track. But CFAR's actual machinery (reference cells around a range/Doppler cell) assumes a spatially distributed sensor field, which a single fixed Hall sensor doesn't have. Treat this as a useful principle, not an applicable algorithm.
Slide-by Hall sensing (industrial application notes, not tracking theory). This is the most directly on-point literature found: Hall-effect manufacturers publish characterized curves for exactly your physical configuration — a magnet moving past a fixed sensor at some air gap — showing that peak flux at a given air gap is a single, predictable point on the manufacturer's head-on flux curve, not something that has to be discovered empirically from scratch. Manufacturers can provide head-on flux density curves that, once the air gap is fixed, allow the peak flux density for the slide-by configuration to be located as a single point on that curve.
Railway application: this bears directly on the presently-unresolved "how do we even know the maximum-flux point" question. Rather than treating it as a pure empirical unknown, it may be partially derivable from the magnet's and sensor's published specs at the known air gap, then verified against actual logged data — cheaper than committing to a full high-rate oscilloscope build immediately.
Track-before-detect (TBD) — flagged as not a good fit. TBD methods (particle filters, dynamic programming over image frames) exist for the opposite problem: an unknown number of low-SNR targets that are individually invisible in any single frame and only become detectable by integrating many frames/images together, at real computational cost (research systems typically use GPUs). Your Hall pulse near a magnet is a strong, single-event signal, not a sub-noise-floor signal needing multi-frame integration, and an ESP32 doesn't have TBD's assumed compute budget. Do not import this.

5. Risks, limitations, likely failure modes
Waveform instability across speed is the biggest risk to matched filtering: if the pulse shape stretches meaningfully between PWM 50 and PWM 120, a single template (or even a template bank) adds real firmware complexity for uncertain benefit.
The "clutter" analog on this railway is largely unverified. Radar/sonar clutter theory assumes a known statistical background (Rayleigh, Weibull, etc.); nothing in your logs yet establishes what a spurious in-gate response actually looks like on NGR — you may be building gating rules against a clutter model you haven't measured.
Sensor drift/temperature can shift the actual peak-flux point session to session even if the manufacturer curve is a good starting estimate — the curve is a prior, not a guarantee.
Provisional-count ambiguity (Question 12) is a real design fork: an SPRT-style accumulation approach only helps if you're comfortable with a marker occasionally being provisionally counted before full confirmation; if the operator's preference is that the count never moves until fully confirmed, none of the sequential-evidence machinery is needed at all, and this becomes a much simpler system.
Importing tracking-literature complexity you don't need is itself a failure mode — JPDA, CFAR clutter maps, and TBD all solve harder problems (multiple targets, unknown counts, sub-noise-floor signals) than TEMPLATES has, and adopting their full machinery risks over-engineering an ESP32 firmware budget for a single-target problem.
6. Techniques that don't fit, and why
Track-before-detect — wrong problem shape (unknown continuous low-SNR scene) and wrong compute budget (GPU-class).
Full JPDA / multi-hypothesis tracking — solves multi-target ambiguity; TEMPLATES has one target by construction, so this is only worth revisiting if double-candidate-in-gate events turn out to be a real, recurring failure mode.
Clutter-map CFAR — assumes a spatially distributed sensor array; you have one fixed sensor per Hall installation.
7. Smallest experiments before choosing an architecture
These are investigation suggestions, not proposals to build anything yet:
Pull existing HallProbe logs (even at the current ~9–10 Hz) and check whether the shape of a magnet excursion looks similar across different passes at similar speed — a cheap first look at whether matched filtering is even plausible, before any new hardware.
Get the magnet and Hall sensor part numbers and air gap, and see whether a manufacturer head-on flux curve exists — a much smaller task than building a new oscilloscope rig, and it directly informs the maximum-flux question.
Using only existing logs, look at whether the near-baseline gap within one magnet's excursion is measurably different from the gap between two separate magnets, even at low sample rate — this was Question 9's aim and may be partially answerable without new data collection.
8. Questions for the operator
Given the boundary of one question at a time — starting with the one that gates the most other choices:
Do you want the system to ever advance the marker count on a "provisional" basis (hard gates passed, timing/pattern confirmation still pending), or should the count only ever move once a hit is fully confirmed? This decides whether any sequential-evidence-accumulation approach (SPRT-style) is worth pursuing at all, versus a simpler hard-gate-then-wait design.
