TEMPLATES Research Brief: Target Acquisition in Noisy Sensor Environments
Investigation only — no code/spec changes. This brief informs discussion; it is not a design decision.
Executive Summary
TEMPLATES reframes marker detection as target acquisition on a known graph, not general classification. The closest real-world analog isn't radar or sonar — it's ETCS/PTC balise correlation in railway signaling: a train with known position, direction, and a "link list" of upcoming beacons expects one specific transponder next, and rejects anything that doesn't match. Radar/sonar contribute the statistical vocabulary (matched filtering, gating, sequential confirmation) but their multi-target machinery (JPDA, MHT) is overkill here — you have exactly one hypothesis at a time, which is a much easier problem than what most tracking literature solves.

1. The TEMPLATES problem, plain language
The locomotive always knows, in principle, which one magnet it should hit next — because it knows where it last confirmed, which way it's oriented, and which way it's moving, and the route map is fixed. The Hall sensor doesn't need to classify every blip; it needs to answer one question repeatedly: "Is this response the specific magnet I'm expecting, or is it something else?" Everything that isn't that one magnet — sensor noise, a stray field, a duplicate reading of the same magnet, electrical interference — is clutter to be rejected without touching position or count. This is a 1-of-1 confirmation problem, not a search among many candidates.
(This restates the operator's traffic-signal analogy from Q2 — established in this repo's interview record, not my inference.)
2. Relevant target-acquisition principles from established sources, and 3. how each maps to the locomotive
a. Matched filtering — SOURCE FACT. A matched filter maximizes SNR by correlating a received waveform against a template of the expected signal shape; detection performance depends on signal energy, not raw amplitude. Medium: RF Chronicle, Liquid Instruments, Emergent Mind. → Application: If a stable per-magnet excursion shape exists (rise time, peak-to-baseline duration), correlating against it — rather than a bare threshold crossing — would be more robust to sensor gain/alignment drift than the current first-crossing method. Assumption required: the excursion shape is repeatable enough across speed and orientation to serve as a template; this is unverified in this railway (Q9/Finding: available Hall logs are ~9–10 samples/sec, too sparse to characterize shape).
b. CFAR (constant false alarm rate) thresholding — SOURCE FACT. Radar sets detection thresholds adaptively from local clutter statistics rather than a fixed value, to hold false-alarm probability constant as background conditions change. MathWorks CFAR, ScienceDirect. → Application: QUORUM's recomputeThresholds() already re-centers on a rolling baseline median (128 samples/500 ms) — this is a primitive form of adaptive thresholding, a fact observed in this repo (firmware/QUORUM/QUORUM.ino:579-587). CFAR's contribution to TEMPLATES would be treating the minimum flux gate the same way: adaptive to slowly-drifting baseline/gain rather than a single fixed count. Assumption: clutter/noise floor drifts slowly relative to magnet transits — plausible but untested here.
c. Validation gating (target tracking) — SOURCE FACT. A validation gate is a region (e.g., derived from predicted state and its uncertainty) inside which a measurement must fall to even be considered for association with a track; nearest-neighbor gating is a hard accept/reject, PDA is a soft weighted combination. USyd/ACFR gating paper, MathWorks assignment methods. → Application: This is the closest formal description of what the interview record's "hard gates" already are — polarity, minimum-time, minimum-flux together define a validation gate in (time × polarity × flux) space. A response outside the gate is rejected outright, matching Q6/Q11 (wrong polarity, too early, too little flux = "not the target, must not move the count"). This is largely a restatement in different vocabulary, not a new capability — the operator's design already independently arrived at gating.
d. Balise/ETCS correlation (railway-specific) — SOURCE FACT, most directly relevant. In ETCS, linked balises give the onboard system a "link list" — the identity of and distance to the next expected beacon — so the system knows what to expect and can flag a missing balise as a fault rather than silently drifting; balise crossings are also used to correct accumulated odometry error. Balise linking overview, ETCS architecture sources above. → Application: This is structurally almost identical to TEMPLATES: known next target from a link list, distance-based expectation window, and — importantly — a "missing expected marker" fault state, not just an "unexpected response ignored" state. TEMPLATES's design record doesn't yet address what happens if the physically-expected magnet never fires (magnet failure, sensor fault) — ETCS practice treats that as a distinct alarm condition worth considering (see §8).
e. M-of-N track confirmation logic — SOURCE FACT. In radar tracking, a tentative detection is promoted to "confirmed" only after M of the last N updates associate with it; confirmed tracks are deleted/coasted after repeated misses. Radartutorial: Track Initiation, MathWorks Track Logic. → Application: This maps directly onto the interview's open Q12 — whether a response passing the three hard gates but lacking timing/pattern confirmation should move the count provisionally, subject to later confirmation, or hold. M-of-N is the formal name for exactly that unresolved design choice; existing literature doesn't resolve which answer is right for this system, only that both provisional-and-revisable and hold-until-confirmed are recognized, named strategies with known tradeoffs (provisional = faster response, more revert risk; hold = safer, laggier).
f. Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT) — SOURCE FACT. Wald's SPRT accumulates a likelihood ratio observation-by-observation and stops as soon as it crosses an accept or reject threshold, minimizing expected samples for a given error rate; applied in radiation/anomaly detection to flag a "different from background" state with as few samples as possible. ScienceDirect SPRT overview, Emergent Mind. → Application: Relevant only if flux is sampled fast enough to accumulate multiple readings across one excursion before deciding it's the target (relates to Q7/Q8's unresolved "when does an excursion close" question). At the current ~9–10 Hz Hall sampling (observed fact, this repo), there may be too few samples per excursion for SPRT to add anything over a simple peak-hold. This needs the resolved-vs-unresolved oscilloscope question (Q9/Q23) before SPRT is even evaluable.
g. Hysteresis / Schmitt-trigger debounce — SOURCE FACT. Separate rising/falling thresholds prevent chatter from small fluctuations near a single threshold. EDN: Switch Debouncing, Hackaday. → Application: Already present in QUORUM (northEnter/northExit, HALL_DEADBAND_COUNTS, HALL_ENTRY_MARGIN_COUNTS — observed fact, QUORUM.ino:579-583). This solves chatter within one excursion, not the identity question TEMPLATES is about — worth naming explicitly so it isn't mistaken for solving target acquisition.

5. Risks, limitations, likely failure modes
Garbage-in from the input boundary (Q1/Q2 unresolved): every technique above assumes there's something meaningful to gate on per excursion. If the raw-sample vs. permissive-collector question isn't settled, the excursion boundary itself is undefined, and flux/timing gates have no stable object to measure.
False confidence from adaptive thresholds: CFAR-style adaptation can itself mask a genuine problem (e.g., a failing magnet or a sensor drift) by "adapting around" it rather than flagging it — this is a documented CFAR weakness in clutter-limited (not just noise-limited) environments (ScienceDirect).
Missing-target blind spot: none of the interview's hard-gate logic (Q6/Q11) currently addresses what happens if the expected magnet simply never arrives (bent magnet, dislodged, sensor fault). Balise systems treat this as an explicit alarm; TEMPLATES's current framing ("responses that don't fit are noise") only describes handling wrong responses, not absent ones.
Provisional-count reversal risk (Q12): if a provisional advance is later invalidated, the operator/display state must be able to walk it back cleanly — a UX and logging risk, not just an algorithmic one.
Sparse sampling undermines shape-based methods: matched filtering and SPRT both lose their advantage if the ESP can't sample fast enough to resolve the excursion shape — this is an open hardware/firmware question (Q9/Q23), not a software-architecture one.
6. Techniques that appear unsuitable, and why
JPDA / Multi-Hypothesis Tracking (MHT): these solve multi-target data association — many simultaneous tracks competing for many simultaneous measurements. TEMPLATES has exactly one target hypothesis active at a time (the physically-next magnet); importing multi-target machinery would add complexity with no corresponding problem to solve. This is a case where terminology from another field does not transfer, per the assignment's explicit caution.
Track-before-detect (TBD): designed for targets so weak they're individually undetectable and must be integrated over many scans before a track can even be initiated (e.g., stealth targets in clutter). Nothing in this railway's evidence suggests magnet responses are anywhere near that weak — Hall excursions are apparently well above baseline when correctly positioned. Applying TBD here would be solving a problem this system doesn't have.
General waveform classification (e.g., ML shape classifiers): the operator explicitly rejected this framing in Q2 — it's not "what does this waveform look like," it's "is this the one target I'm expecting." Classification techniques optimized for distinguishing many signal classes are the wrong tool for a binary in-context/not-in-context decision.
7. Smallest experiments needed before choosing an architecture
These are ordered by dependency, not importance — later ones are blocked on earlier ones:
High-rate Hall oscilloscope capture (Q10B/Q23, deferred not approved) — without this, no excursion-shape, maximum-flux-timing, or event-closure question can be answered empirically. This is the actual bottleneck blocking almost every other item on this list.
Event-closure rule from real waveform data (Q9) — once (1) exists, measure longest near-baseline gap within one magnet vs. shortest gap between two separate magnets, to bound the closure rule.
Maximum-flux point stability check — is the flux peak's timing repeatable enough across speed/orientation to serve as the timestamp (open question #5 in the record)? This determines whether flux-peak timing is usable at all, independent of the flux-magnitude gate.
Minimum-flux gate derivation method — Q7/Q22 leave this undefined; needs a decision on what "minimum flux for this target" means (per-magnet? per-locomotive? measured how?) before it can be a hard gate.
Only after 1–4: the previously-scoped PWM/speed remeasurement campaign (Q19–21, 32 laps) makes sense to run, since it depends on knowing what point in the excursion timing is even being measured against.
8. Questions requiring operator judgment
(Presenting the first one now, per your instruction to ask one at a time.)
Missing-target handling: the interview record resolves what happens when a Hall response arrives but fails the gates (it's noise, ignored). It doesn't yet address the reverse case — the expected magnet's physical minimum-arrival window opens and closes with no qualifying response at all. Balise-based systems treat this as a distinct fault condition rather than staying silent.
Do you want TEMPLATES to eventually distinguish "no candidate arrived in the expected window" as its own evidence category (worth flagging, logging, or alarming on), or should that stay entirely out of scope for now and just be silence — i.e., the system simply keeps waiting with no separate signal?
