NGR NAVI_RVM — Independent Magnetic Shape Recognition Research

Assignment for Codex

Repository:  ⁠ninobur/esp-loco-control

Scope: Research, statistical model construction, comparison, and evaluation only.

No firmware development or modification is authorized.

1. Objective

Determine whether a statistical model of the characteristic shapes produced by the Lowline’s 171 magnetic mile markers can reliably distinguish magnetic ensembles (MM) from intervening baselines (BL) in continuous Hall recordings.

Compare two approaches:

Model A — General composite

Construct a statistical shape model using all 171 magnetic ensembles and their associated baseline regions.

The objective is to recognize what a magnetic ensemble generally looks like, rather than identify an individual magnet by its historical waveform.

Model B — Geographic-specific composite

Construct individual statistical shape models for MM050–MM070, using repeated recordings of each magnet and its associated baseline regions.

Determine whether geographic-specific models provide a meaningful recognition advantage over the general composite.

The central research question is whether Model A can recognize magnetic ensembles and baselines as reliably as Model B, without requiring individual magnetic portraits.

2. Architectural rationale

Previous NAVI development repeatedly converted magnetic recognition into collections of numerical measurements and acceptance gates.

These included Hall amplitude thresholds, duration limits, PWM-derived movement estimates, predicted magnet arrival times, waveform-fidelity scores, and probabilistic geographic tracking.

Although individually defensible, these mechanisms repeatedly produced false negatives, missed counts, or incorrect geographic interpretations.

The operator’s hypothesis is simpler:

Magnetic fields produce characteristic, recognizable waveform shapes. Baselines produce a different class of shapes. A recognizer should distinguish those classes directly.

The existing 20-ms Hall recordings show prominent magnetic hills and valleys, associated lobes, and relatively level intervening regions.

The purpose of this investigation is to determine whether those structures can be recognized statistically without requiring the exact amplitude, width, or historical appearance of an individual magnet.

This is a test of the fundamental recognition mechanism, not a navigation-system reliability or recovery study.

3. Governing recognition concept

The fundamental pattern is:

MM₁ → BL → MM₂

Each magnetic ensemble includes its principal excursion and associated lobes and shoulders.

The baseline is a recognizable intervening structure. It may be gently curved rather than perfectly horizontal.

Successive units overlap:

MM₁ → BL → MM₂ → BL → MM₃

MM₂ belongs to both adjacent units but contributes only one geographic advancement.

The model is retrospective. Recognition need not occur at the first departure from baseline. A completed magnetic structure may be evaluated before geographic identification.

The known geographic map supplies expected polarity and sequence.

The statistical recognizer determines whether the observed waveform is a magnetic ensemble or baseline. The geographic map determines which MM is expected.

These responsibilities must remain separate.

5. Model construction

Model A — General composite

Build a statistical representation of the magnetic ensemble class from all 171 magnets.

Include the characteristic relationships among:

* Principal positive and negative excursions.
* Associated leading and trailing lobes.
* Shoulders and ordinary waveform variations.
* Transitions between magnetic ensembles and baseline.

Construct a corresponding statistical baseline model.

Account for polarity by appropriate normalization or separate positive/negative shape families.

Evaluate whether the recordings support one general family or distinguishable families, potentially including disk and ballast magnets.

Do not assume separate families are required.

Model B — Geographic-specific composite

Construct statistical models for individual magnets MM050–MM070 from repeated recordings.

Use the same underlying recognition methodology as Model A.

The principal difference between A and B must be the reference model, not the detector, preprocessing, or scoring algorithm.

This is necessary for a fair comparison.

6. Width and amplitude independence

The same physical magnetic structure may produce a broader or narrower time-domain waveform depending on locomotive speed.

Its amplitude may also vary.

Recognition must not depend on estimating locomotive speed or predicting when the next magnet will arrive.

The recognizer should accommodate ordinary width and amplitude variation through its shape representation.

Width may be measured after recognition and may eventually provide useful speed information. It must not serve as a geographic qualification gate.

Any normalization or alignment must preserve the meaningful structure of the ensemble.

In particular, do not permit a matching algorithm to skip significant lobes, compress an entire magnetic excursion into a small shoulder, or stretch insignificant features into a convincing match.

The previous Codex RVM investigation demonstrated why unconstrained waveform alignment is unacceptable.

7. Explicit prohibitions

These prohibitions are governing requirements, not suggestions.

Do not:

1. Reuse or modify Claude’s existing probabilistic recognizer.
2. Reuse or modify Codex’s previous RVM alignment kernel.
3. Implement a PWM-based position estimator or geographic tracker.
4. Use IR, PWM, locomotive speed, elapsed travel distance, or predicted magnet arrival to qualify magnetic recognition.
5. Implement individual amplitude, width, or duration gates as substitutes for statistical shape recognition.
6. Define magnets solely through Hall threshold crossings.
7. Define baselines solely through elapsed duration or mapped distance.
8. Require individual magnets to reproduce their own historical waveform portraits.
9. Introduce geographic recovery, missed-magnet compensation, competing recovery hypotheses, or failure-management machinery.
10. Implement navigation-triggered STOP, E-STOP, redeclaration, or motor-control behavior.
11. Add automatic operation, station stopping, dispatcher functions, or CTO integration.
12. Modify operational firmware, locomotive configuration, Pi services, or the existing dashboard.
13. Retune recognition parameters separately for individual evaluation recordings.
14. Add vestigial compatibility code, dormant subsystems, or speculative future features.

Do not replace a prohibited mechanism with an equivalent mechanism under a different name.

Numerical calculations are permitted and necessary for statistical shape recognition. The prohibition concerns independent measurement gates acquiring authority over recognition.

8. Independent research requirement

This investigation must be independent of Claude’s implementation.

Codex may inspect prior research, recordings, and documented failures to understand the problem and avoid known defects.

However, it must develop its own statistical shape-recognition method.

Do not copy Claude’s model architecture, likelihood calculations, or implementation.

Do not use the previous RVM dynamic-programming alignment kernel as the foundation.

The objective is an independent engineering assessment of the operator’s hypothesis.

9. Evaluation methodology

Use the same source recordings and evaluation partitions for both models.

Separate reference construction from evaluation.

Where recordings have previously been examined during NAVI development, disclose that fact. Do not describe reused evidence as a fresh independent holdout.

Evaluate:

Criterion	Required comparison
Magnetic recognition	Correctly recognized ensembles
Baseline recognition	Correctly identified intervening baselines
False positives	Baseline structures incorrectly identified as magnets
False negatives	Magnetic ensembles not recognized
Lobe grouping	Associated lobes incorrectly identified as separate magnets
Polarity	Correct identification of principal polarity
Ensemble boundaries	Beginning, end, and midpoint accuracy
Width variation	Recognition across recorded operating speeds
Amplitude variation	Recognition of taller and shorter versions of valid magnetic shapes

Include MM050–MM070 as a focused comparison region.

Evaluate Model A on additional geographic regions to determine whether its generalization extends beyond the comparison segment.

Do not use Model B outside its defined geographic coverage.

Report recognition decisions against existing annotations, identifying where those annotations are uncertain.

Important methodological requirement

A recognizer must not appear more accurate merely because it refuses to classify difficult observations.

Report unresolved observations separately and include them in the evaluation.

10. Required evidence

Provide:

* A description of the statistical representation used by each model.
* The training and evaluation datasets.
* The observed variation within magnetic ensembles and baselines.
* Quantitative separation between the two classes.
* Recognition results for Models A and B.
* Representative successful and unsuccessful waveform comparisons.
* The effect of amplitude and width variation.
* Any evidence that disk and ballast magnets require different shape families.
* An explanation of how the algorithm preserves complete ensembles and associated lobes.

Identify cases where magnetic and baseline models overlap substantially.

If the statistical models cannot distinguish the classes reliably, report that result.

Do not introduce another recognition or recovery subsystem to conceal the limitation.

12. Success criterion

The investigation succeeds if it provides credible evidence answering:

Can NAVI reliably distinguish magnetic ensembles from baselines by recognizing their characteristic shapes, without individual measurement gates or movement-based geographic qualification?

A successful result need not demonstrate a complete navigation system.

If the general composite performs comparably to geographic-specific references, prefer the general model for its simplicity.

If geographic-specific models perform substantially better, explain why and identify what information the general composite lacks.

If neither performs adequately, report the failure without designing recovery machinery.

We are not asking you to build another navigation framework.

Recognize what a magnet looks like—not when it should arrive, how far the locomotive should have traveled, or whether it resembles its individual historical portrait.
